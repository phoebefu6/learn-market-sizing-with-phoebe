Learn Market Sizing with Phoebe · Analyst session 4 of 6

Ranges, not points

A market size is evidence for a decision, and a single number is the weakest form that evidence can take: it hides which assumptions it rests on and how far each could be wrong. Tonight the size becomes a range, the range is held up against the line where the decision flips, and a tornado chart shows which one assumption moves the answer most. That bar is the next thing to measure, and saying so is the most useful sentence an analyst can put in a sizing brief.

Part 1 · covers why a point is the wrong answer

A point looks safe because it hides its own doubt

5 min live

Business session 1 asked the owner for a threshold before anyone sized anything: for Kettle & Co, entering Japan online is worth it at about 10 billion yen a year of SAM. That threshold is constructed for teaching. Against it, the point 19.63 looks like a comfortable yes. The ranges tell a different story.

LiveWhat the point hides, and what the line reveals3 min▶

The point. 19.63 billion yen a year is the top-down chain with every constructed share at its middle. It reads as twice the threshold. Nothing on the slide says that two of its four links are assumptions, or how far they could move.

The top-down range. Carry both shares to their lows together and to their highs together and the same chain prints 10.90 to 31.72. The whole range sits above a line at about 10, but the low end clears it by less than one billion yen, and "about 10" was never precise to the billion.

The bottom-up range. Built from Kettle & Co's own pilot inputs, the bottom-up prints 13.06 at the middle and 7.11 to 21.55 as a range. That range crosses the threshold. If the true value sits in its lower part, the decision is no.

So the honest answer to "go or no-go?" is neither. It is "measure more, and here is the one input to measure first". A range wholly above or below the threshold decides; a range across it tells you where to spend the next week.

Self-studyWhy "measure more" is a real answer, not a dodge2 min▶

Leaders sometimes hear a range as an analyst refusing to commit. Turn it around: a point that sits on the wrong side of the truth commits the business to a decision the evidence never supported. "Measure more" is only a dodge when it comes without a target. Paired with the tornado in Part 3, it becomes specific: which input, how wide it is today, and what a cheaper or better source would be.

It also tells you when to stop. Once a range sits wholly on one side of the threshold, further precision changes nothing about the decision, and the analyst can say so.

Real world

The slide that said "19.6". Kettle & Co's head of data presents 19.6 billion yen against a 10 billion line, and the room relaxes. The marketing lead asks what the low end is. Nobody knows. The rewritten slide shows both ranges and the line, and the meeting ends with a measurement plan instead of a launch date. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Part 2 · covers ranges as corners, and their limits

Lows together, highs together, and what that does not tell you

5 min live

The simplest honest range gives every assumption a low, a middle and a high, then carries them together: all lows for the bottom of the range, all middles for the point, all highs for the top. Those are the corners of the space of possible answers. They are easy to explain and deliberately wide.

LiveLow, middle, high, carried together3 min▶

Every constructed input gets three values, and each value gets a reason. For Kettle & Co's top-down: premium share of leaf spend 0.25 / 0.33 / 0.40, share bought online 0.22 / 0.30 / 0.40. For the bottom-up: households that buy the category online 3.5% / 4.5% / 5.5%, spend per order 2,100 / 2,400 / 2,700 yen, orders per buyer a year 2.8 / 3.5 / 4.2. All constructed.

The two real links, 34,553,907 households and 5,737 yen of leaf spend per household, keep their single value: they are measured, and the sampling error of the survey table is not published in the table used, so it is not quantified here. Carry the constructed lows together and the top-down prints 10.90; the highs together print 31.72; the middles print 19.63.

LiveThe limits of corners2 min▶

Wide on purpose. A corner is the case where every assumption missed the same way. That can happen, especially when one cause moves several inputs (a weak economy lowers premium share and orders together), but it is the edge of the possible, not the middle of the likely.

Silent on likelihood. A corner range says nothing about whether 12 is more plausible than 28. Do not draw a bell curve on top of it, and do not write "90 percent confident" next to it. Nobody measured a probability, so none goes on the page.

Self-studyAlternatives some teams prefer: scenarios and sampling3 min▶

Scenarios. Instead of mechanical corners, write two or three named stories, a cautious one and a bold one, each with inputs that belong together. A scenario keeps inputs coherent (a world where few households buy online is probably also one where online orders are less frequent), and leaders find stories easier to argue with than corners. The cost: the stories are chosen, so a team can choose comfortable ones.

Sampling, often called Monte Carlo. Give each input a distribution, draw thousands of combinations, and read off the spread of results. It shows which part of the box is crowded, which corners cannot. The cost is that it needs a distribution for every input and a view on how inputs move together, and the output is only as real as those choices. A percentile computed from invented distributions is still invented.

Neither is wrong. Some teams start with corners because they are transparent, then move to sampling once they have real data behind the input distributions. Others refuse sampling until then. Pick the method your evidence can support, and say which you picked.

Real world

The confidence interval nobody measured. An analyst at Kettle & Co runs a quick simulation with a normal distribution around every middle and reports a precise-looking probability that the market clears 10 billion. The BD lead quotes it to the board. Asked where the distributions came from, the analyst says they were defaults. The next version shows corners and the tornado, and says plainly that no probability has been measured yet. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Part 3 · covers sensitivity and the tornado

Which one assumption moves the gap most?

5 min live

Corners say how wide the answer is. Sensitivity says why. Move one input from its low to its high while every other input sits at its middle, record what happens, and repeat for each input. Here the thing measured is not either size but the gap between them: top-down divided by bottom-up, 1.50x at the middle. Sort the results widest first and you have a tornado.

LiveReading the five bars3 min▶

Why the gap, not the size. Each size on its own moves with every input. The gap between them is what tells you the two methods disagree, and an input that closes the gap is a candidate explanation for the disagreement. At the middle of every input the top-down is 1.50 times the bottom-up.

The order. Share bought online is widest: at its low the gap falls to 1.10, at its high it climbs to 2.00. Then premium share (1.14 to 1.82), households that buy online (1.93 at its low, 1.23 at its high), orders per buyer a year (1.88 to 1.25), and spend per order (1.72 to 1.34), the narrowest.

The direction. The two top-down inputs widen the gap as they rise, because they inflate the top-down. The three bottom-up inputs narrow it as they rise, because they inflate the bottom-up. Read the ends, not just the widths: a bar tells you which way the input would have to move to bring the methods together.

LiveWhy the widest bar is the next thing to measure2 min▶

Measurement costs time and money. The widest bar is where a better number buys the most: narrow the online share and the gap's possible spread shrinks more than for any other input. Narrowing spend per order, the narrowest bar, would barely move the conversation even if it were cheap.

Width is not the only test. Ask also how cheaply the input can be narrowed. If two bars are close in width and one can be checked from Kettle & Co's own orders table in an afternoon, do that one first. The tornado ranks impact; you add cost.

Self-studyThe caution: inputs that move together2 min▶

A tornado holds every other input at its middle. Real inputs do not always sit still while one moves. If the households that buy online are fewer than assumed, the ones who do buy may also buy more often, so two bottom-up bars could partly cancel. Or one cause could push several inputs the same way and the joint effect would be larger than any single bar.

One-at-a-time sensitivity cannot see either case. Use it to rank where to look, not to state the combined uncertainty; the corners in Part 2 and, if your evidence supports it, scenarios or sampling cover the joint movement.

Real world

Three weeks on the wrong input. Kettle & Co's analysts spend three weeks refining spend per order from pilot baskets, because the data was already in the warehouse. The tornado, drawn afterwards, shows it was the narrowest bar. A one-week retailer panel query on the online share would have moved the gap more. They now draw the tornado before choosing what to refine. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Homework

Before Analyst session 5

30 min
  1. Give every assumed link in your own sizing a low, a middle and a high, each with one line of reason. If you cannot write a reason for a low or a high, the range is a guess about a guess; say so.
  2. Ask the decision owner for the threshold in their own language, then place your range against it: wholly above, wholly below, or across.
  3. Draw a tornado by hand for your two or three widest assumptions: move each from low to high with the others at middle and record the result. Rank them.
  4. Write your range sentence in the step 4 format, ending with the one input you will measure next, how, and by when.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The bottom-up range is 7.11 to 21.55 and the decision threshold is about 10 billion yen. Why is the honest answer "measure more"?

A range wholly above or below the line decides; a range across it tells you to narrow the input that moves it most before anyone commits.

2 · In the tornado, share bought online is the widest bar, gap 1.10 at its low and 2.00 at its high. What does that tell you?

Width ranks impact on the gap. Picking the value that makes the methods agree would be choosing the answer first; measuring it is the job.

3 · What is a limit of one-at-a-time sensitivity?

A tornado ranks where to look. Joint movement belongs to corners, scenarios or, with real distributions behind it, sampling.
