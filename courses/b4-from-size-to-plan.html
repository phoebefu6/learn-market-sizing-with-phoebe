From size to plan

A market size is a ceiling, not a forecast. Kettle & Co now has a signed brief: premium loose-leaf in Japan, bought online, 10.90 to 21.55 billion yen a year. The board wants to know what Kettle will actually sell, and when. Tonight turns the size into a plan in three moves: a share reached over years rather than on day one, a check of that share against what comparable entrants actually reached, and an evidence gate in Kettle's own numbers that has to open before anyone spends as if the share were already won. Kettle & Co, its plan and its bars are constructed; the households and the tea spend behind the size come from Japan's own statistics.

Part 0

Where we start

Three sessions in, Kettle & Co has the decision written down, the category chosen, the deck read with a sceptic's eye and the brief signed in two halves. What it does not have yet is a plan. The gap between the two is where most sizing work goes wrong: a careful range on page one becomes "we will win 10 percent" on page two, and the budget is set as if that 10 percent arrives next quarter. Tonight is about the page two that a board can trust. No code. The Analyst track builds the same ramp and the collision test in the browser.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A one-page plan that writes the size as a range, ramps the share year by year with a reason for each year, checks the year-5 share against comparable entrants, and puts a gate of four of your own numbers between the pilot and the year-2 budget. It ends in one paragraph you could read to a board without anyone mistaking a claim for a measurement.

Part 1 · covers penetration and ramp

A share is reached over years, not on day one

5 min live

Kettle's year-5 plan is 10 percent of SAM: 1.96 billion yen a year against a serviceable market of 19.63. That number is a destination, not a starting point. A plan that spends as if year 5 had arrived in year 1 hires a team for revenue it will not see for four years. So the plan needs a ramp: a share for each year, each with a reason, rising under a ceiling it never touches.

LiveWhy the year-5 cell is the least reliable number in the plan3 min▶

It is the furthest from anything measured. Year 1 can lean on the pilot: real visitors, real first orders. Year 5 leans on four years of assumptions stacked on top of each other: how fast new customers arrive, how many come back, what rivals do, whether the market itself grows or shrinks. Each layer adds error, and none of the layers has been observed yet.

It is the number the whole plan is reverse-engineered from. In most plans the year-5 share is written first ("we want 10 percent") and the ramp is drawn backwards to reach it. That makes it the most wished-for cell, not the most evidenced one.

It is also the number the budget is quietly built on. Hiring, media and stock are often sized for the destination. That is why the gate in Part 3 sits between year 1 and year 2: the budget follows the measured years, not the hoped-for one.

Self-studyAdoption curves, as a shape and nothing more2 min▶

Frank Bass's 1969 paper in Management Science (reported here, not read at source) gave planners a familiar shape for how a new product spreads: a slow start among the few who try new things first, an acceleration as others copy them, then a flattening as the people left to convert get scarce. Kettle's ramp borrows only that shape: small steps early, bigger steps in the middle years.

The shape is a realism check, not a forecast. No parameters from the paper are used here, and any growth rates you see on a ramp are planning assumptions. Analyst session 5 uses the same curve to ask whether a plan's middle years climb faster than adoption plausibly allows. Fitting curves to real sales and forecasting them belongs to Timeseries Forecasting ↗.

Self-studyWhy the sketch holds SAM flat1 min▶

The figure keeps the ceiling at 19.63 for all five years so the ramp is the only thing moving. A real plan would ask whether premium loose-leaf bought online is itself growing in Japan. That is a forecasting question, and it is a separate line on the plan with its own range, never folded silently into the share.

Real world

The plan that arrived in year 1. Kettle & Co's marketing lead takes the 1.96 billion yen year-5 figure and divides the annual media budget against it, because "that is the market we are going after". In the first year the online pilot brings in roughly a tenth of what the budget assumed, and the cost per customer looks catastrophic. Nothing was wrong with the customers; the budget had been sized for year 5. Constructed Kettle & Co example; the shape is common.

Part 2 · covers base rates

Check the plan against what others actually reached

5 min live

Every plan is written by people who believe in it. A base rate is the outside view: in this kind of category, what share did comparable entrants actually reach by their fifth year? In a crowded category most entrants end small. A plan above what comparable entrants reached is not wrong, but it is a claim, and a claim needs evidence that this entrant is different.

LiveWhat a base-rate check actually asks3 min▶

One question: of the companies that entered a market like this one, what share did they actually reach, and how long did it take? Not the winners you remember, but the comparable set: similar product, similar channel, similar size at launch, including the ones that closed.

Then compare. If Kettle's 10 percent sits inside what comparable entrants reached, the plan is ordinary, which is a compliment. If it sits above, the plan has to say why Kettle is different: a channel nobody else has, a cost advantage, a customer base it already owns. "We are better" is not a reason; every entrant believed that.

Where the evidence comes from. Company filings, trade association reports, retailer rankings, the data team's own scan of rival storefronts. No single published base rate covers every category, and the honest answer for a niche one is often "we found five comparables, not fifty". Write the count next to the band; a band built on five companies is a weaker check than one built on fifty, and the board should know which it is looking at.

Self-studyThe collision test: the base rate at market level2 min▶

A base rate checks one plan against history. The collision test checks every plan in the market against each other. In the constructed Kettle market, Kettle plans 10 percent of SAM, Entrant B 18, Entrant C 22, Entrant D 15, and the incumbents expect to hold 55. Add them up and the plans claim 120 percent of a 19.63 billion yen market: 23.55 billion yen of planned revenue, 3.93 billion of it with no customer to buy it.

No single plan looks absurd on its own. Together they cannot all come true, and someone's year-5 cell is going to miss. Analyst session 5 builds the test live in the browser. The plans are constructed; the arithmetic is the engine's.

Real world

Individually rational, collectively impossible. Aswath Damodaran (Musings on Markets, 2015 and 2019 posts, read at source) took a set of listed online advertising companies, worked out the revenue each one's market price implied for a future year, and added them up. The total came out bigger than his own generous estimate of the whole market. Each company's story was plausible; the sum was not. Kettle's version is constructed: when the BD lead lines up Kettle's 10 percent beside what the three named rivals have told investors, the market runs out before the plans do. Constructed Kettle & Co example for the second half.

Part 3 · covers an evidence gate before the share figure

Own numbers first, share figure second

5 min live

A market size says how much there is to win. It says nothing about whether this team, with this product, can win it. That second question is answered by the company's own numbers from the pilot: do people convert, how long do they take, does the money come back, do they come back. Until those numbers clear a bar written in advance, the share figure stays behind a gate, and so does the budget that assumes it.

LiveRoberge's stance, and why it belongs in a sizing session3 min▶

Mark Roberge, who wrote The Sales Acceleration Formula (2015) and has written since about scaling go-to-market teams, is reported to argue a simple order of operations: do not pour money into sales and marketing because the market is big; pour it in when your own numbers show the engine works. Paraphrased here from secondary accounts, not quoted, and no figures are his.

The four numbers that usually stand for "the engine works": win or conversion rate (do the people you reach actually buy), cycle (how long from first contact to first purchase), payback (how long until the margin on a customer repays what it cost to win them), and repeat or retention (do they come back). A market size touches none of them. That is exactly why it cannot be the reason to scale.

For a direct-to-consumer brand like Kettle, "win rate" becomes conversion and "cycle" becomes days to a first order. For Kettle's office tea idea, run through a BD team, they would be the classic sales versions: deals won out of deals pitched, weeks from first meeting to signature.

Self-studyWho writes the bars, and when2 min▶

Before the pilot starts, not after it ends. A bar written after the results are in tends to land just below them. Kettle's bars are agreed at the board meeting that approves the pilot.

The bet owner proposes, the data team checks it can be measured. The same split as the brief in Business session 3: the BD or marketing lead says what level of conversion would justify scaling; the head of data says whether the pilot will produce enough orders to tell. A bar of 3 percent conversion means little if the pilot only reaches a few hundred visitors.

What the gate protects against. Spending as if the market share were already won. The commonest failure in a plan is not a wrong size; it is a right size spent against too early.

Real world

The gate that held. Kettle & Co's online pilot clears three bars in its first year: conversion, days to first order and payback. Repeat comes in under the bar: too few first-time buyers reorder within 90 days. Instead of releasing the full year-2 budget, the board releases a small one aimed at the repeat problem alone (subscriptions, a refill reminder) and re-tests in six months. The 1.96 billion yen year-5 figure is not cut; it is simply not spent against yet. Constructed Kettle & Co example.

Build-along

Turn Kettle's size into a plan you can defend

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

No code, one page. Work through Kettle & Co's plan, or your own if you brought a size from Business session 3. Five steps: the size as a range, a ramp with a reason for every year, the base-rate check, the gate, and the paragraph for the board. Do step 1 together, steps 2 to 4 in pairs, and read step 5 aloud. Kettle's filled example is constructed; your shares and bars will differ, and they should.

Step 1 · Write the size as a range, not a point. Copy row 6 of the brief from Business session 3 as it was signed. Kettle's is the overlap of two methods, 10.90 to 21.55 billion yen a year, with 19.63 as the top-down middle. The plan quotes the range; any single figure used later says which point of the range it is.

★ Step 1 · the sizesize (from the signed brief): ________ to ________ a year middle used for the ramp: ________ (say which method it comes from) signed by: ________________ (the evidence owner) Kettle example: 10.90 to 21.55 bn yen a year; ramp uses SAM 19.63

Step 2 · Draw a 5-year ramp, with a reason for every year. One share per year and one line on why it is that number and not double. A year with no reason is a year drawn backwards from year 5. Kettle's shares below are constructed; multiply each by the SAM middle to get revenue.

Step 4 · Set the evidence gate. Four of your own numbers, each with a bar written before the pilot ends. Name who measures each one and from which table. Kettle's bars are constructed for a direct-to-consumer pilot; a B2B team would use deal win rate and weeks to signature.

★ Step 4 · the gatecheck bar measured from today win or conversion rate ________ ________________ ______ cycle ________ ________________ ______ payback ________ ________________ ______ repeat or retention ________ ________________ ______ gate opens when: all four clear (anything less: targeted fix, re-test) Kettle example (constructed): conversion >= 3% of trial visitors buy orders and site visits cycle first order within 14 days orders table payback ad cost repaid within 6 months orders + media spend repeat >= 30% reorder within 90 days orders table

Step 5 · Write the plan statement for the board. One paragraph. The range, the ramp with its destination labelled as a plan, the base-rate status, and the gate. Read it aloud; if anyone hears a forecast where you wrote a claim, rewrite it.

★ Step 5 · the plan statement"We size ________ (category) in ________ (place) at ________ to ________ a year. We plan to reach ____ % of it by year ____, ramping from ____ % in year 1. Comparable entrants reached ________; our plan is [inside / above] that, because ________. Year 1 is a pilot. The year-2 budget is released only when ________, ________, ________ and ________ clear the bars set today." Kettle example (constructed plan): "We size premium loose-leaf tea bought online in Japan at 10.90 to 21.55 billion yen a year. We plan to reach 10% of the 19.63 middle, 1.96 billion yen, by year 5, ramping from 1% in year 1. The comparable-entrant check is still open, so 10% is a claim, not a forecast. Year 1 is a pilot; the year-2 budget is released only when conversion, days to first order, payback and 90-day repeat clear the bars above."

Keep the page next to the brief. The brief from Business session 3 says what the market is and who signed each half. The plan page says what Kettle will do about it, and when it is allowed to spend. Together they are two pages a board can read in five minutes and argue with usefully.

Homework

After the Business track

30 min
  1. Find a plan with a year-5 share in it, yours or one you were shown. Write next to it whether years 1 to 4 each have a reason, or whether they were drawn backwards from year 5.
  2. Name five comparable entrants for that category and find what share any of them reached. Write down how many you could actually find a number for, and where.
  3. Write the four gate bars for that plan: win or conversion rate, cycle, payback, repeat. For each, name the table it would be measured from, and whether that table exists today.
  4. Rewrite the plan statement in one paragraph using the step 5 template, and send it to the person who owns the bet. Ask them one question: which bar would they be least comfortable being held to?
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Business session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Kettle's plan ramps from 0.20 billion yen in year 1 to 1.96 in year 5. Why is the year-5 cell the least reliable number on the page?

Year 1 leans on the pilot. Year 5 inherits every earlier year's error and is often the wish the plan started from, which is why the budget should not be built on it.

2 · What does a base-rate check ask of a plan?

The outside view compares a plan with what similar entrants really reached, including the ones that closed. A plan above the band is not wrong, but it owes the board a reason.

3 · Kettle's year-2 budget is held behind a gate of four of its own numbers. What does the gate protect against?

A market size says how much there is to win, not whether this team can win it. The gate makes the company's own numbers earn the budget the share figure implies.
